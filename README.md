The interface is pretty straight forward. 

Threat Event Frequency means how often a threat acts against something valuable.

A threat can be a person, a group, a system, or even nature.

Examples include:

A hacker trying to break into an account
An employee clicking a phishing email
A storm damaging a data center
A system failing
A worker accidentally deleting a file
The key question is:

How often does the threat show up or take action?

For example, imagine a school email system gets phishing emails every day. That means the threat event frequency is high. The threat is showing up often.

Now imagine a small local business gets a serious hacking attempt only once every few years. That threat event frequency is lower.

In FAIR, vulnerability does not just mean “a weakness exists.”

It means the chance that a threat event becomes a real loss event.

A threat event is when something tries to cause harm.
A loss event is when it actually succeeds and causes damage.

For example, a hacker may try to guess your password. That is a threat event.

If your password is strong and you use multi-factor authentication, the hacker probably fails. No loss happens.

If your password is “password123” and you have no extra protection, the hacker may succeed. Now it becomes a loss event.

FAIR looks at vulnerability by asking:

If the threat acts, how likely is it to succeed?

That depends on things like:

How strong the attacker is
How good your defenses are
How easy the target is to attack
How well people follow security rules

Loss Magnitude means how much money is lost when a bad event happens.

For example, if a company has a data breach, the cost might include:

Paying workers to fix the problem
Hiring investigators
Notifying customers
Paying legal fees
Paying fines
Losing customers
Losing sales
Repairing damaged systems
Losing trust
FAIR separates Loss Magnitude into two main types:

Primary Loss
Secondary Loss

Primary Loss
Primary Loss is the direct loss that happens to the organization.

This is the immediate cost of the event.

Examples include:

Lost employee time
Cost to repair systems
Cost to replace hardware or software
Cost to investigate the incident
Lost revenue from downtime
Emergency response costs
Imagine an online store goes down for four hours.

During that time, customers cannot buy anything. The company loses sales. Workers have to fix the issue. The IT team may work overtime.

Those are primary losses.

They happen directly because of the event.

Secondary Loss
Secondary Loss happens because other people react to the event.

This can include customers, regulators, lawyers, business partners, or the public.

Examples include:

Government fines
Lawsuits
Customer refunds
Loss of customers
Bad news coverage
Reputation damage
Higher insurance costs
Loss of business partners
For example, imagine a company loses customer data.

The primary loss includes fixing the system and investigating the breach.

The secondary loss might include customers leaving, regulators giving fines, and lawyers filing lawsuits.

Secondary loss can sometimes be much larger than primary loss.
